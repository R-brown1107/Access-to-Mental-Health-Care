The Problem

01 / THE PROBLEM

Mental health affects way more people than most realize. I think one of the biggest problems is that mental health is still treated differently from physical health. People are often comfortable talking about being physically sick and the struggle it brings, but talking about struggling mentally is often much harder. Especially in men, because many men grow up being taught to tough it out and that they should be able to get through issues alone, which can ultimately lead to more mental health issues than may have been present before.

Harvard's Health Care Policy program describes mental illnesses as being "just as frequent as the common cold," yet they are "more rarely discussed, diagnosed, and treated." (Harvard)

That difference is important. If mental illness is common, then getting help for it should not feel unusual or embarrassing. With how common it is to struggle with it, shouldn’t care for mental health be more accessible and accepted? Accessibility starts with recognizing mental health care as a normal part of health care rather than something people should only seek when their problems become severe.

23.4% of U.S. adults experienced mental illness in 2024. However, only 52.1% of adults with mental illness received treatment. (National Alliance on Meental Illness)

More than half receiving treatment might sound like progress, but it also means nearly half did not receive treatment. If someone needs care but cannot receive it, can we really call that care accessible?

02 / THE REALITY

Too many barriers stand between people and care.

Even when people recognize that they need mental health support, getting that support can be difficult. The Association of American Medical Colleges identifies several barriers to mental health care, including provider shortages, insurance problems, cost, and difficulty navigating the health care system.

Provider Shortages

One of the biggest barriers is simply finding someone who can provide care. The AAMC reports that 129.6 million people lived in federally designated mental health professional shortage areas in 2021.

That number is hard to ignore. Someone may realize they need help, decide to seek assistance, but still struggle to find a provider. This shows why telling people to “just get help” is not as easy as it sounds. People may want help, but they are not able to easily find someone to provide it.

Cost and insurance

Even when a provider is available, paying for care can create another barrier. The AAMC found that 30% of adults with a mental illness and an unmet need for services reported not receiving care because their insurance did not cover mental health services or did not pay enough for them.

This is one of the clearest examples of why simply having mental health services available is not enough. Physical health care already comes with many costs, however; it is more widely covered and accepted in comparison with mental health care. The cost is often very high, and insurance coverage is very low. People should not have to choose between basic needs such as providing for their family and receiving care for mental illness that affects them everyday.

Knowing where to start

Access can also become difficult before someone ever reaches a provider. The AAMC found that one-third of adults with a mental illness and an unmet need for services said they did not receive care because they did not know where to go.

This can be easy to overlook. When someone is already struggling, asking them to navigate a complicated health-care system can become another obstacle. Many people in the U.S. are not educated on the resources available to receive help for their mental health. Since many are not educated, they do not know where to turn to find help when they need it, and likely have not looked into it before they end up needing it. Knowing how to find help should be widely taught and necessary for all people to know.

03 / WHY IT MATTERS

When care is out of reach, the problem doesn't go away.

Harvard's Health Care Policy program argues that mental disorders are often treated as “acute, episodic ailments instead of chronic illnesses.”

The most important part is not that people who are already beyond a crisis have help, but rather that those who might be helped and are ready to get help are not forced to wait until the latest possible moment. People should not be required to be pushed to their limit, to reach a breaking point, before they receive help. If help for those with mental health issues becomes more accessible, there would be far less people to reach a state of crisis.

In addition, NAMI reports that the average delay between the start of mental illness symptoms and treatment is 11 years.

This should not be the case. If mental disorders are potentially chronic illnesses, like many physical disorders, there should not be so much stigma around seeking help. Those 11 years are years of potential suffering, and something that could have been prevented but wasn’t. It makes me wonder why the default assumption is that mental health treatment is an emergency measure. Mental health services and care should be available before somebody is beyond help. The help should be right there as a possibility for every person that realizes they need it, regardless of anything else. Not a last resort, a final option, or something to be feared. Help should be the first step. Not the last.
